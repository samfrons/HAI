# Protection Officer Playbook

Protection work runs on trust — a survivor's trust that their information stays contained, a community's trust that "confidential" means confidential. That trust is the reason this playbook leads with a boundary, not a use case: **treat any AI chat, including HAI, as a channel that could someday be read by someone other than you.** Never put a real case, a real name, or a real identifying detail into it. Everything below assumes that boundary holds.

## Where AI genuinely helps in this role

1. **Looking up referral pathway structure and standards** — what a referral pathway document should contain, what IASC guidance says about timelines — as a starting point, not a live directory.
2. **Checking a program design against protection mainstreaming principles** (Sphere's Protection Principles, CHS commitment 4) using only generic, non-case program details.
3. **Drafting blank case-note or intake templates** — headers and structure only, never filled with real content.
4. **Drafting generic, non-individualized talking points** for explaining complaints and feedback mechanisms to a community.
5. **Summarizing publicly available IASC or cluster guidance** on protection mainstreaming, GBV, or child safeguarding minimum standards.

## Where AI should NOT be used

- **Never paste actual beneficiary names, ages, locations, case numbers, or incident details into any prompt** — even a first name plus a village plus an incident type can re-identify someone. If you're unsure whether something counts as identifying, don't paste it.
- **Never for beneficiary eligibility or protection risk-level decisions.** That judgment belongs to a trained case worker who has seen the actual case.
- **Never as the sole source for a live referral pathway.** Pathways change; always confirm the current version with the actual cluster or Area of Responsibility (AoR) focal point before referring someone.
- **Never for safety planning content tied to an individual survivor.** Use AI only for generic protocol or template drafting — a real safety plan is caseworker-only work.
- **Never assume de-identification is complete just because you removed the name.** Age, location, incident type, and date together can still identify a person even with no name attached.

## Example prompts

**1. (Beginner)** "What are the minimum actions for protection mainstreaming according to Sphere's Protection Principles?"
*Why it works: a general standards lookup with no case data involved, answerable with a citation.*

**2. (Beginner)** "Draft a blank case-note template structure — headers only, no example content — covering presenting concern, actions taken, and referral status."
*Why it works: explicitly restricts the request to structure, not content, which keeps identifiable information out by construction.*

**3. (Intermediate)** "Summarize the IASC guidelines on GBV referral pathway design — what are the core components a referral pathway document should have?"
*Why it works: asks for structural guidance rather than a specific, current pathway with real service names or locations, and is answerable from a citable source.*

**4. (Intermediate)** "Using CHS commitment 5 (safe reporting of concerns) language, draft 3 generic talking points staff can use to explain our complaints mechanism to a community, without referencing any individual case."
*Why it works: ties the ask to a named commitment and explicitly excludes individual cases from the output.*

**5. (Intermediate)** "Check this generic distribution plan description [paste, no beneficiary data] against protection mainstreaming principles and flag anything that could create protection risks — crowding, visibility, unsafe access routes."
*Why it works: the input is a general program plan, not case data, and the task is bounded to flagging risks, not adjudicating them.*

**6. (Advanced)** "Act as a reviewer: given this de-identified, hypothetical case-type scenario (not a real case) [paste a synthetic example], what referral options exist under the cluster system for this kind of situation, and what does IASC guidance say about referral timelines?"
*Why it works: the explicit "hypothetical, not a real case" framing lets you reason about pathway logic without any real person's data ever entering the prompt.*

**7. (Advanced)** "Draft a one-page protection mainstreaming checklist for a cash assistance program in [context], citing Sphere and CHS sources for each checklist item."
*Why it works: a per-item citation requirement makes every checklist line independently verifiable, and the task needs no case-level data at all.*

**8. (Advanced)** "Compare our draft child safeguarding SOP [paste, redacted of names] against the CHS commitments and IASC guidance on Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (PSEA), and list gaps as a table."
*Why it works: names the correct framework (PSEA, not a generic "safeguarding standard"), requires redaction up front, and asks for a structured, checkable gap list.*

## Verify before you act

- Before sending anything to AI, re-read it once specifically hunting for names, ages, locations, case numbers, or photos. If in doubt, don't paste it — rephrase generically instead.
- Confirm any referral pathway or service list with the current cluster or AoR focal point — an AI summary can be outdated or too generic to act on.
- Route anything touching an actual case, safety plan, or risk assessment to a supervisor or caseworker. An AI draft is never the case record.
- Re-check PSEA and GBV minimum-standard references against the current IASC guidance version before finalizing an SOP or training — this guidance is periodically revised.
